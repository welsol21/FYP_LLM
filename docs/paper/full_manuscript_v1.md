# Mendeleev's Method in the Age of AI: The Sequence Pattern Hypothesis Across Language and Weather

## Abstract

Recent advances in artificial intelligence and machine learning have given researchers practical access to new ways of identifying stable regularities in complex data at a scale that was previously difficult to achieve. This creates an opportunity not only to analyse observed phenomena more effectively, but also to search within them for regularities that may reflect deeper underlying laws of nature, including laws not yet formally described.

This paper develops that possibility in the form of the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis. The hypothesis states that a complex system may be studied by decomposing its observable manifestations into analytical units, extracting abstract patterns from those units, and learning the sequences in which such patterns recur, combine, and evolve.

The hypothesis is examined through two case studies. In the linguistic case, language is approached as a compositional system in which higher-order constructions emerge from the recursive combination of lower-level analytical units, represented here as Recursive Linguistic Elements. In the weather case, each observational channel is first analysed separately and divided into intervals within which one local equation continues to describe the behaviour of that channel. A segment ends at the point where that equation stops matching the observed behaviour, and a new segment begins with a new equation. In a second pass, the segmentations from different channels are aligned on a shared temporal axis, introducing further boundaries where needed and refining the timeline until each final segment is one in which every channel can still be described by its own local equation. In other words, the boundary of any final weather segment lies at the point where at least one of the equations describing the channels within that segment ceases to hold.

Across both domains, the reported results suggest that useful predictive and interpretive information can be preserved not only in raw observations, but also in sequences of abstract patterns derived from them. The paper therefore argues that, in the age of AI, Mendeleev's method may be repeated in a new form: by identifying analytical units, extracting abstract patterns, studying the sequences in which they recur, and using those sequences as a basis for prediction and discovery.

## 1. Introduction

Science has approached a point at which many systems can no longer be studied adequately through direct inspection of raw observations alone. Their observable behaviour is difficult to analyse not only because it is complex in itself, but because the number of relevant parameters and interacting factors grows explosively with each step deeper into the internal organization of the system. Under such conditions, further progress depends not only on the new analytical capabilities made practical by recent advances in artificial intelligence and machine learning, but also on the accumulated scientific experience of studying complexity through recurring units, regularities, and their organization, as seen in such cases as Mendeleev's periodic table and the discovery of DNA structure.

One of the clearest historical examples of such an approach is Mendeleev's periodic table. Its significance lay not simply in the classification of known elements, but in the discovery that abstract properties could be organized into a repeating order, and that this order could be used to predict what was still missing. The scientific force of the table was therefore not only descriptive, but predictive.

The present work proposes that this general scientific logic can be repeated, in a new form, under the conditions created by contemporary AI and ML. More specifically, it advances the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis: that complex systems may be studied by identifying their analytical units, extracting abstract patterns from those units, and learning the sequences in which such patterns recur, combine, and transform.

A central part of this proposal is the distinction between open and closed systems. An open system is a system as it exists in nature, where observable content, including the current state of the system, gives rise to patterns and to sequences of patterns. A closed system is a reconstructed, formalized, or generative version of such a system, in which the direction is reversed: already extracted and organized pattern sequences give rise to new content, including interpretations, predictions, reproducible states, and new acts of generation. In this sense, a closed system may take the form of a model, an experiment, a technology, a formal law, or even a scientific discipline itself. This content-pattern duality is important because it links the phase of observation and abstraction with the phase of inference and generation. A useful analogy is foreign-language learning: the language itself exists as an open system with its own content, while the learner infers an internal version of that language from extracted patterns and pattern sequences, and then begins to generate new linguistic content from that internalized system. That content is not identical to the original open system, yet it remains understandable to other participants in it and, in the end, naturally becomes part of that open system through the new speaker's individual variant of the same language.

The hypothesis is examined in this paper through two case studies that differ sharply in their observable material but share the same underlying logic. The first is language, represented through Recursive Linguistic Elements as a compositional system of analytical units and higher-order constructions. The second is weather, where observable channels such as temperature, pressure, and wind speed are segmented into time intervals within which these channels can be described by equations that have valid solutions inside the boundaries of each interval.

Taken together, these two cases are used not to claim a complete theory of all complex systems, but to demonstrate that the same methodological logic can operate across domains with very different forms of observable content. The central claim of the paper is that analytical units, abstract patterns, and sequences of patterns may provide a generalizable basis for prediction, interpretation, and discovery in systems whose raw manifestations would otherwise remain too complex to organize directly.

The contribution of this paper is therefore twofold. First, it formulates the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis as a general methodological proposal about how complex systems may be studied through analytical units, abstract patterns, and their sequences. Second, it provides two cross-domain case studies showing how this logic can be implemented in practice in both linguistic and meteorological settings.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. First, the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis and the distinction between open and closed systems are defined in conceptual terms. The two case studies are then presented in turn, followed by a cross-domain discussion of their shared logic, their limits, and their implications for future scientific use of AI in the study of complex systems.

## 2. Background and Positioning

The present paper is positioned between two well-established ways of studying complex systems. One of them seeks explanation through first principles, that is, through governing relations derived from theory and observation. The other seeks predictive power through end-to-end learning from raw data. Both approaches have produced important scientific results. However, between them there remains room for an intermediate logic in which observable content is first reorganized into analytical units, abstract patterns, and their sequences before being used for inference.

In the first-principles tradition, the aim of science is to explain a system by identifying the variables that matter, the relations that govern them, and the laws through which the observed behaviour can be derived. This remains one of the strongest forms of scientific understanding because it links explanation to mechanism and grounds prediction in explicit theoretical structure. At the same time, this route becomes increasingly difficult when the system under study contains too many interacting parameters, too many levels of organization, or too little theoretical transparency for a complete derivation to be available. Even in fundamental physics, where first-principles reasoning has been extraordinarily powerful, major difficulties remain. Quantum uncertainty (the impossibility of assigning exact values to some pairs of physical quantities at the same time), the observer problem (the question of whether observation itself affects the state of the system), the measurement problem (the difficulty of explaining how one definite outcome emerges from multiple quantum possibilities), and thought experiments such as Schrödinger’s cat (which illustrates the tension between quantum superposition and ordinary macroscopic reality) all show that a theory may predict with great precision while still leaving unresolved what exactly is happening in nature and how observation is related to the state of the system itself.

The second major route is end-to-end black-box learning. Here the system is not first decomposed into explicit analytical units or patterns, but is instead presented with raw or minimally processed input from which useful internal representations are learned automatically. This strategy has shown remarkable power in tasks where predictive performance matters more than transparent structure, especially when very large volumes of data and computation are available. Its main strength lies in flexibility and scale: it can capture complex dependencies without requiring that the researcher specify in advance how the system should be structurally organized.

At the same time, the black-box route has an important limitation for the kind of problem considered in the present paper. When inference is learned directly from raw input, the resulting prediction may remain weakly connected to the structural organization that actually matters for interpretation. This was precisely the difficulty encountered in the linguistic case. In the project experiments, a DeBERTa baseline on the corresponding full CEFR-ladder setting remained near 16.1% accuracy, with predictions collapsing largely into a single class. In such settings, raw end-to-end inference does not provide sufficiently relevant outputs. What is needed is a representation in which the structural basis of the prediction remains explicitly connected to the content being analyzed.

For this reason, the present paper is concerned with an intermediate possibility. It does not begin from the assumption that a full first-principles derivation is already available, but neither does it rely only on raw-input fitting. Instead, it asks whether observable content can first be decomposed into analytical units, whether these units can be transformed into abstract patterns, and whether the resulting patterns can then be placed on a temporal scale and studied as sequences and flows. In this way, the object of inference is no longer only the original content itself, but also the ordered pattern structure that emerges from it.

This intermediate logic is not without historical precedent. Mendeleev’s periodic table remains one of the clearest scientific examples of how analytical units can be organized through abstract properties into an ordered structure that becomes predictive. The table did not merely classify known elements. It revealed recurring order, exposed gaps, and made it possible to infer what had not yet been observed directly. In this sense, its scientific power lay not only in description, but in the discovery of structure through abstraction and order.

A related, though different, example is DNA. Here too, scientific understanding depends not only on the existence of physical material, but on the ordered sequence structure through which that material becomes biologically meaningful. What matters is not merely the presence of molecular components, but the way they are arranged and interpreted as a sequence. This example should not be pushed too far, but it reinforces the broader point: in some domains, order and sequence are not secondary features of scientific description, but central carriers of structure and inference.

The present paper should therefore be read neither as a rejection of first-principles science nor as a dismissal of end-to-end learning. Its claim is more limited and more specific: under the technological conditions created by contemporary AI and machine learning, an older scientific logic of decomposition, abstraction, ordering, and inference may be repeated in a new methodological form. In some cases, this makes it possible to construct a scientifically useful intermediate layer between raw observable content and inference. The Sequence Pattern Hypothesis is proposed as a way of describing and testing this possibility across different domains.

## 3. The Sequence Pattern Hypothesis

At its most general level, the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis states that some complex systems can be studied not only through raw observations or fully specified governing equations, but also through the identification of analytical units, the extraction of abstract patterns from those units, and the study of the sequences in which such patterns recur and interact.

The hypothesis does not deny the value of first-principles science, nor does it reject the use of modern machine learning. Instead, it proposes an intermediate scientific logic: before attempting either full mechanistic derivation or end-to-end statistical fitting, one may first seek the recurring units and regularities through which the observable behaviour of the system is organized.

In this framework, an analytical unit is the minimal recurring element through which the system becomes tractable for study. The unit need not be physically fundamental in an ontological sense; rather, it is fundamental in a methodological sense, because it is the smallest element at which stable regularities can be identified, compared, and reused in further analysis.

An abstract pattern is a higher-order description extracted from such units. It does not preserve every surface detail of the original observation, but retains those features that are stable enough to recur across different instances and informative enough to support interpretation, comparison, or prediction.

A pattern sequence arises when these abstract patterns are not considered in isolation, but in the order in which they appear, combine, and transform over time or within a structured system. The central assumption of the hypothesis is that this sequential organization is itself a carrier of knowledge, because it preserves regularities that are not visible at the level of isolated units alone.

The hypothesis therefore implies a characteristic sequence of scientific work: observable content is decomposed into analytical units; analytical units are mapped into abstract patterns; abstract patterns are organized into sequences; and those sequences are then used as the basis for interpretation, prediction, reconstruction, or discovery. In this sense, the sequence is not merely a record of what happened, but a structured object from which new knowledge may be inferred.

This does not mean that every complex system will admit a useful decomposition of this kind, nor that every abstraction will preserve the right information. The hypothesis is therefore not a universal guarantee, but a methodological claim: where stable analytical units, abstract patterns, and informative pattern sequences can be found, they may provide a workable path between raw complexity and scientific inference.

The purpose of the present paper is not to prove this hypothesis in full generality, but to clarify its logic and examine its plausibility in two sharply different domains. By doing so, the paper seeks to show that the same sequence-based mode of abstraction can operate both in a recursive symbolic system such as language and in a continuous observational system such as weather.

## 4. Context-Pattern Duality

In this paper, an open system is understood as a system as it exists in nature, while a closed system is understood as a reconstructed, formalized, or generative version of that system. The distinction between them is therefore not a claim that two different realities exist, but a claim about two different modes in which the same reality may appear in scientific work. In the open mode, the system confronts the observer as external content from which patterns still have to be extracted. In the closed mode, those patterns have already been organized into a form that can produce interpretation, prediction, or new content. Because this organization is always carried out by a particular observer, model, or community of observers, different closed versions of the same open system may coexist. In this sense, the distinction is related to the observer problem known from physics, especially from quantum theory, where the observed system cannot be treated as fully independent of the act of observation: what exists in nature as a field of possible states becomes more definite only through a particular interaction, measurement, or interpretive framework.

From this perspective, open and closed systems should not be understood as mutually isolated domains, but as phases of one cognitive movement. In the open phase, the task is to extract recurring units and regularities from natural content. In the closed phase, the task is to use the resulting pattern organization as a source of inference, reconstruction, controlled generation, prediction, experimental intervention, and, in some cases, discovery of what has not yet been directly observed.

This is precisely the point at which the analogy with Mendeleev becomes methodologically significant. The periodic table was not only a way of arranging known elements, but a closed pattern system from which missing elements could be inferred before they were empirically confirmed. In this sense, the transition from open observation to closed pattern organization is not merely a change in representation; it is the point at which description becomes discovery.

The same logic applies, in a weaker but still meaningful form, to learned or engineered systems. Once recurring patterns have been stabilized into a closed representational form, they can be used not only to reproduce known behaviour, but to generate new admissible states, new interpretations, or new structured hypotheses that remain connected to the original open system from which they were derived.

A useful illustration is foreign-language learning. The language itself exists as an open system with its own content, structures, and constraints. The learner extracts recurring patterns from that open system and organizes them internally into a closed system from which new linguistic content can be generated. That generated content is not identical to the full open system, but insofar as it remains understandable to other speakers and constrained by the same language, it re-enters the open system as part of its ongoing life.

The two case studies developed in this paper can be understood within exactly this dual framework. In language, natural utterances are first encountered as open content and then transformed into Recursive Linguistic Elements and structural pattern sequences. In weather, natural observations such as temperature, pressure, and wind speed are first recorded separately from one another and then transformed into joint equation-based segments and the sequences of those segments, that is, regime sequences. In both cases, the open phase provides the material from which patterns are extracted, while the closed phase provides the organized form from which prediction, interpretation, or discovery becomes possible.

## 5. Case Study I: Language

The linguistic case study is not an external illustration added after the fact, but the original domain in which the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis first took practical shape. The ELA project and its associated research line began as an attempt to help learners work with authentic English without simplifying the source material, yet the effort to produce reliable linguistic explanations led to a deeper methodological problem: the system needed a stable way to identify and reuse the recurring structural regularities of language.

The immediate obstacle was that linguistic explanation could not be made sufficiently reliable at the level of raw lexical content alone. Free-form generation was capable of producing plausible text, but it was not consistently anchored to the grammatical organization of the sentence itself. This made it necessary to move from words as isolated items toward a representation in which structural relations could be preserved, compared, and reused across different utterances.

This requirement led to the use of Recursive Linguistic Elements as the analytical unit system of the linguistic domain. In ELA, each sentence is represented not as a flat string, but as a recursive structure composed of sentence, phrase, and word nodes, each of which can carry grammatical, structural, and pedagogical attributes. In this way, the sentence becomes decomposable into units that are both linguistically meaningful and methodologically reusable.

These units are not important only because they divide the sentence into parts. Their importance lies in the fact that they support abstraction: each node may be described by attributes such as its type, grammatical role, part of speech, tense, aspect, mood, CEFR level, and other features that are more stable than the specific lexical surface. Through this shift, the relevant signal moves from isolated words toward relations between abstract structural properties.

Once the sentence is represented in this way, different utterances can be recognized as instances of the same abstract pattern even when their lexical content differs. What recurs is not necessarily the same sequence of words, but the same structural configuration distributed across analytical units. This makes it possible to treat language not only as lexical content, but as a system of abstract patterns and pattern sequences.

Within the open phase of the linguistic case, authentic English sentences are observed as concrete linguistic content and represented through the RLE contract as structured analytical units. Within the closed phase, the experimental line developed inside ELA studies how different representations of the same sentence affect downstream tasks: whether a richer input in the form of the RLE contract improves pedagogical note selection, whether structural features support CEFR classification better than raw text, and whether templated or direct note formulations generalize more reliably as the set of target note classes becomes more complex. In this respect, the relevant linguistic work was not unrestricted explanation generation, but a controlled comparison of representational forms and their effect on classification, interpretation, and later note realization.

The first important result was obtained in pedagogical note selection. When the input representation was changed from a more superficial sentence description to a richer input in the form of the RLE contract, the proportion of cases in which the correct note appeared in first position increased from 0.3519 to 0.3928, while the proportion of cases in which the correct note appeared within the top three candidates increased from 0.6037 to 0.7700. This showed that a more structured sentence representation could improve interpretive stability even without requiring a more complex model.

An even stronger result appeared in the CEFR line. When difficulty classification relied on structured features extracted from the contract representation of the sentence, performance became exceptionally high: one tabular CEFR classification experiment reached about 99.6% test accuracy, and a later joint CEFR-grammar profiling experiment reached 100% test accuracy on its benchmark. By contrast, a DeBERTa baseline evaluated on the corresponding full CEFR-ladder setting remained near 16.1% accuracy. Although these results are specific to the project's datasets and label conditions, they show that, in this setting, the structural representation carried a much stronger signal for classification than a generic neural text encoder alone.

A third line of evidence came from experiments on the target space of pedagogical notes themselves. When direct text-based notes were compared with templated notes containing placeholders, the direct notes achieved higher first-choice accuracy at smaller and medium class counts, but their quality degraded more rapidly as the number of unique note types increased. Templated notes began from a weaker absolute level, yet their behaviour under growth of the target space was more stable. This result mattered because it showed that abstraction influences not only immediate accuracy, but also the way model behaviour changes as the task becomes structurally harder.

Negative evidence was also important. In one early experiment, the target space was compressed too strongly: after balancing, the dataset was reduced to 511 rows and only 10 unique targets, and both tested model variants failed to produce useful note predictions. This showed that abstraction does not help simply by making the task smaller. It helps only when it preserves the structural regularities of the data without removing distinctions that are still needed for interpretation.

Taken together, these results support a clear conclusion for the linguistic domain. The relevant gains did not arise from treating language as an unlimited collection of surface examples, but from decomposing observable linguistic content into analytical units, representing those units through abstract patterns, and organizing the resulting structure in forms that support stable interpretation across changing lexical content. In this respect, the linguistic line inside ELA provides the first implemented evidence for the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis: language becomes more tractable when observable content is reorganized into analytical units, abstract patterns, and pattern sequences that can be reused under lexical variation.

## 6. Case Study II: Weather

The weather case study serves as the first cross-domain test of the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis outside the linguistic domain in which it originally emerged. This matters because weather forecasting differs fundamentally from language in both material and form: instead of symbolic recursive structures, it presents continuous observational signals evolving over time. If the same logic of analytical units, abstract patterns, and pattern sequences remains useful in such a setting, the hypothesis gains stronger methodological support.

In the weather project [REF-WEATHER], the observable content consists of recorded channels such as temperature, pressure, and wind speed. The central methodological move is not to model these channels directly as raw time series, but to analyse each channel separately and segment it into time intervals within which the channel can be described by a local equation. In a second pass, the segmentations obtained from different channels are aligned on a shared temporal axis and refined into joint segments whose boundaries are determined by the point at which at least one of the channel equations ceases to hold. These final segments function as analytical units, from which weather regimes and their sequences can be constructed.

The choice of local equations depends on the physical character of the channel. In the reported experiments, temperature was segmented using six equation families: constant, linear, exponential, harmonic, linear-harmonic, and damped-harmonic. Pressure and wind speed were segmented using three simpler families: constant, linear, and exponential. The purpose of this design was not to reproduce full atmospheric physics, but to capture recurring local forms of behaviour in a compact and interpretable way.

Applied across six years of observations from a single meteorological station, Knock Airport, this two-pass procedure produced a joint corpus of 10,055 segments with a mean duration of 5.3 hours. At this point, the raw stream of hourly measurements had been transformed into a sequence of interpretable analytical units, each representing a period during which all observed channels remained jointly describable within the boundaries of the segment. The forecasting task could therefore be reformulated from predicting raw values directly to predicting the succession of such regimes.

The resulting regime sequence was then used as the input language of the forecasting model. Rather than learning directly from hourly values alone, a transformer was trained to operate over the succession of analytical descriptors associated with the segments and to predict the next regime sequence. In this way, the model learned not only local behaviour types, but also the order in which such types tend to follow one another over time.

The strongest reported result was obtained for pressure forecasting at the 12-hour horizon. On the February 2026 evaluation period, the model achieved a pressure MAE of 0.89 hPa, compared with 1.35 hPa for a naive persistence baseline, corresponding to a 34% improvement. This result is especially important because it shows that, even without explicit atmospheric physics or spatial information, a sequence model operating on analytically segmented regimes can recover predictive structure that is not captured by simple continuation of the last observed state.

This pattern was also visible in temperature and wind speed. For temperature, the ensemble reached MAE values of 1.42 C over hours 1-12, 2.31 C over hours 13-24, and 1.79 C over hours 25-168, with an overall 168-hour MAE of 1.80 C; that is, the forecast differed from observed temperature by about 1.4 C, 2.3 C, 1.8 C, and 1.8 C on average over those intervals. For wind speed, the corresponding MAE values were 2.48 kt, 1.67 kt, and 6.22 kt, with an overall 168-hour MAE of 5.62 kt, meaning that the forecast differed from observed wind speed by about 2.5, 1.7, 6.2, and 5.6 knots on average. For additional evaluation context, these results were also compared with two operational numerical weather prediction systems used here as reference forecasts: ECMWF IFS 0.25°, the operational forecast system of the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts, and GFS Seamless, the operational forecast product of the U.S. Global Forecast System. This comparison is especially relevant because the present model was developed without the large-scale observational, computational, and operational infrastructure available to the meteorological services behind those systems.

To characterize regime-change timing more directly, a derived turning-point recall was computed on 3-hour smoothed forecast curves using a matching tolerance of plus or minus 6 hours. A change moment was counted as recovered when the forecast contained a turning point within plus or minus 6 hours of the observed turning point.

Taken together with the pressure result, this is notable because these results were obtained without the large-scale observational, computational, and operational infrastructure available to the forecasting services used here for operational comparison. The weather case therefore supports the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis beyond the linguistic domain: useful predictive information is retained not only in raw atmospheric measurements, but also in analytically constructed sequences of local regimes.

## 7. Cross-Domain Synthesis

Taken together, the two case studies suggest that the same analytical logic can operate across domains with sharply different observable content. In both cases, the first step is to identify analytical units and extract from them abstract patterns that fix recurring structure. Once these abstract patterns have been identified, they can be traced as an ordered flow in time: in language, in the time of speech or reading, and in weather, in the time of observed atmospheric change. This makes it possible to define pattern sequences and to examine recurrence, transition, and arrangement as part of those sequences rather than as isolated properties.

At this level of description, both domains admit the same general chain: observable content is decomposed into analytical units, analytical units are described through abstract patterns, and abstract patterns are organized into pattern sequences that support inference. What changes from one domain to another is not the logic of the chain, but the material from which each of its stages is constructed. In language, the units are recursive linguistic elements and the resulting sequences support interpretation, classification, and controlled generation. In weather, the units are equation-bounded segments and the resulting sequences support forecasting. This shared chain is the main reason the two case studies can be treated not as separate illustrations, but as evidence for one cross-domain hypothesis.

At the same time, the comparison also makes clear that the hypothesis does not depend on identical boundary mechanisms across domains. In language, analytical boundaries arise from recursive structural decomposition into sentence, phrase, and word-level units. In weather, analytical boundaries arise where the local mathematical description of at least one observed channel ceases to hold, after separate channel-wise segmentations have been aligned on a shared temporal scale. The boundary rule is therefore domain-specific, but the methodological role of the boundary is the same in both cases: it defines the units from which abstract patterns and their sequences can be constructed. This distinction is important because it shows that the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis is not tied to one particular type of material, but to a general logic of decomposition, abstraction, and ordered pattern formation.

This shared logic also clarifies why the Mendeleev analogy remains methodologically significant. The point of the analogy is not that language or weather reproduce the structure of chemistry, but that predictive and interpretive value can arise when analytical units are arranged into an abstract order that makes missing, recurring, or transitional structure visible. In this respect, the important scientific move lies not only in raw description and not only in full mechanistic derivation, but in constructing an intermediate ordered representation in which patterns and their sequences can be examined and used for inference. The two case studies suggest that such a representation can be built in more than one type of system. For this reason, the present paper treats abstract pattern sequences as a possible basis for interpretation, prediction, and discovery in complex systems.

At the same time, this comparison makes clear the main challenge of the proposed approach. The central difficulty lies not in stating that complex systems may contain abstract patterns and their sequences, but in determining how such patterns are to be identified, bounded, and organized in a scientifically useful way. In different domains, this requires different criteria of decomposition, different boundary rules, and different forms of abstraction. For this reason, the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis should be understood not as a finished solution, but as a research programme centered on the discovery and validation of pattern structure.

Taken together, the two case studies suggest that the scientific value of the proposed approach lies not in a single isolated method, but in a combined methodology. This methodology builds on long-established scientific practices such as decomposition into analytical units, abstraction from observable content, ordered representation, and inference from structured regularities. What is new is that recent advances in AI and machine learning make it increasingly feasible to identify, compare, and use such patterns and their sequences at a scale that was previously difficult to achieve. In this sense, the proposed approach is intended not to replace existing scientific methods, but to extend the scientific toolkit by adding a new way to work with complex systems through abstract patterns and their sequences.

## 8. Discussion

The main value of the proposed approach lies in combining abstract patterns with time and thereby turning them into flows that can be studied, modeled, and used for inference. Once observable content has been decomposed into analytical units and represented through abstract patterns, these patterns no longer remain isolated descriptions. Placed on a temporal scale, they form pattern flows whose recurrence, transition, and organization can be analyzed as structured objects in their own right. In this sense, the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis is important because it proposes a way to move from raw complexity to analyzable flows of patterned structure.

This point also helps explain why the approach may have scientific value beyond the two domains examined in the present paper. If the temporal organization of abstract patterns can be represented as a flow, then the object of study is no longer only the original content, but also the dynamics of the pattern flow itself. Such flows can be compared, classified, segmented, predicted, and related to one another. This opens the possibility that in at least some complex systems, knowledge may be gained not only from isolated states or local descriptions, but from the behavior of pattern flows over time.

A further advantage of this perspective is that it keeps the intermediate structure open to inspection and controlled variation. When inference is based only on raw input and opaque internal representations, it is often difficult to determine what exactly carries the useful signal or how changes in the system affect the result. In the present approach, by contrast, the analytical units, abstract patterns, and their temporal organization remain explicit enough to be examined directly and varied in a controlled way. This does not eliminate error or guarantee correctness, but it makes it easier to analyze where a model succeeds, where it fails, and how changes in parameters, boundaries, or pattern definitions influence the resulting pattern flow and inference.

At the same time, this approach makes available a relatively simple method of parameter approximation. Because the analytical units, abstract patterns, and their temporal organization remain explicit, their parameters can be adjusted iteratively in response to observed mismatch between the model and the natural system. This makes it possible to move step by step toward a closer representation of real behavior rather than relying only on a single fixed description from the start. In this sense, the proposed framework is useful not only for abstraction, but also for gradual approximation to the behavior of complex systems in nature.

For this reason, the proposed approach may be especially useful for systems that are too complex to be understood directly from raw observations, but still contain regular structure that can be decomposed and abstracted. In such cases, recent AI and ML technologies, used in the context of the proposed hypothesis, do not replace scientific method. They strengthen it by making it more practical to identify, trace, and model abstract patterns and their flows in time. The result is not a new science instead of the old one, but an expanded scientific toolkit for working with complexity.

This also helps explain why the proposed framework should not be understood as a competitor to classical scientific approaches. Its role is not to replace physical theory, causal reasoning, or established methods of analysis, but to provide an additional way of organizing complex observable content into forms that are easier to study. Where first-principles models are available, this approach may complement them. Where such models are incomplete or difficult to construct, it may offer a practical intermediate route to analysis, modeling, and inference.

At the same time, it is natural that scientific methods of inquiry also change as the analysis of nature requires increasingly powerful means with each step deeper into its internal organization and as new technological possibilities emerge. The point is not that earlier methods lose their value, but that they are supplemented, extended, and transformed under new conditions. In this sense, the proposed framework should be seen as part of the ongoing evolution of scientific method. Its purpose is to adapt the logic of scientific inquiry to growing analytical demands and to the new practical means made available by AI and machine learning.

## 9. Limitations

The present paper should be read within the limits of its scope. It proposes a cross-domain hypothesis and supports it through two case studies, but it does not claim that the same analytical logic has already been established for all complex systems. The results presented here are sufficient to motivate the hypothesis and to show its practical plausibility in more than one domain, but not to close the broader scientific question. For this reason, the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis is best understood at this stage as a research direction rather than as a completed general theory.

A first limitation concerns the range of empirical support considered in the paper. Only two domains are examined here, and although they differ sharply in their observable content, this by itself is not enough to justify broad generalization. The cross-domain value of the argument lies in showing that the same methodological logic can appear in more than one setting, but the extent of its applicability remains open. Further work is needed to test whether similar pattern-based flows can be identified and used productively in other kinds of complex systems.

A second limitation concerns the dependence of the approach on the quality of decomposition and abstraction. The usefulness of pattern flows depends on whether the chosen analytical units, boundary rules, and abstract patterns preserve enough of the original structure to support meaningful inference. If the decomposition is too coarse, or if the abstraction removes distinctions that still matter, the resulting flow may become too weak or distorted to remain scientifically useful.

A third limitation concerns the domain-specific character of the constructions used in the present work. In language, the analytical units and abstract patterns depend on a particular structural representation of sentences and on the design choices embedded in that representation. In weather, the analytical units depend on the selected equation families, segmentation criteria, and alignment procedure across observational channels. These constructions are scientifically motivated, but they are not unique. Other domains, and even the same domains, may require different analytical designs in order for pattern flows to become informative. Determining such designs is itself a subject of further scientific investigation.

A fourth limitation concerns the depth of explanation that this approach can provide. It helps identify recurring structure, organize it into pattern flows, and use those flows for interpretation or prediction. However, this is still different from explaining why the system behaves in this way at the level of its underlying physical or causal laws. In other words, the method can show that certain regularities exist and can be used, even when the deeper mechanism behind them has not yet been fully derived. For this reason, the proposed framework should be seen as a practical step toward deeper explanation, not as its final form.

The language case also has clear empirical limits. Its results are tied to the quality and structure of the dataset used in the experiments, including the way the data were selected, organized, and labeled. For this reason, the reported gains should be interpreted within that experimental setting rather than as evidence of unrestricted language understanding.

The weather case also remains limited in scope. It is based on a single-station setup, limited channels, and no information about the wider spatial structure of the atmosphere, as well as limited horizon comparison. Its strongest result is a 12-hour pressure forecast that improves on naive persistence, but the overall evidence remains preliminary relative to operational forecasting standards. In this sense, the weather case provides a meaningful non-linguistic test of the hypothesis, but not a full validation of the forecasting approach.

Taken together, these limitations do not invalidate the proposed framework, but they do define its current scientific status. The Sequence Pattern Hypothesis should therefore be treated as a research direction and testable framing, not as a completed general theory of complex systems.

## 10. Conclusion

Complex systems often resist direct understanding at the level of raw observable content alone. As analysis moves deeper into the internal organization of nature, the number of interacting factors grows and the limits of surface-level description become more apparent. This creates the need for methods that do not merely accumulate observations, but reorganize them into forms that are more suitable for interpretation, prediction, and discovery. The present paper has examined one such possibility through the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis.

The main outcome of the present work is the formulation and cross-domain support of the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis as a new methodological possibility for the study of complex systems. The paper suggests that once observable content is decomposed into analytical units and transformed into abstract patterns, the placement of these patterns on a temporal scale makes it possible to study them not as isolated descriptions, but as sequences and flows. It is precisely at the level of these time-ordered pattern flows that complex systems may become more accessible to analysis, modeling, prediction, and gradual scientific refinement. In this sense, the proposed approach is intended not to replace established scientific methods, but to extend the scientific toolkit through a new way of organizing complexity in time.

The present paper has not attempted to prove the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis as a universal law of complex systems. Its aim has been more limited and more precise: to formulate the hypothesis clearly and to examine its plausibility in two sharply different domains. Across both language and weather, the results suggest that observable content may become more tractable when it is decomposed into analytical units, transformed into abstract patterns, and organized into sequences and flows in time. In this sense, the paper argues that analytical units, abstract patterns, and their temporal organization may provide a workable scientific basis for prediction, interpretation, and discovery in systems whose complexity grows explosively as their study moves deeper into their internal organization.
