Science has approached a point at which many systems can no longer be studied adequately through direct inspection of raw observations alone. Their observable behaviour is difficult to analyse not only because it is complex in itself, but because the number of relevant parameters and interacting factors grows explosively with each step deeper into the internal organization of the system. Under such conditions, further progress depends not only on the new analytical capabilities made practical by recent advances in artificial intelligence and machine learning, but also on the accumulated scientific experience of studying complexity through recurring units, regularities, and their organization, as seen in such cases as Mendeleev's periodic table and the discovery of DNA structure.

One of the clearest historical examples of such an approach is Mendeleev's periodic table. Its significance lay not simply in the classification of known elements, but in the discovery that abstract properties could be organized into a repeating order, and that this order could be used to predict what was still missing. The scientific force of the table was therefore not only descriptive, but predictive.

The present work proposes that this general scientific logic can be repeated, in a new form, under the conditions created by contemporary AI and ML. More specifically, it advances the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis: that complex systems may be studied by identifying their analytical units, extracting abstract patterns from those units, and learning the sequences in which such patterns recur, combine, and transform.

A central part of this proposal is the distinction between open and closed systems. An open system is a system as it exists in nature, where observable content, including the current state of the system, gives rise to patterns and to sequences of patterns. A closed system is a reconstructed, formalized, or generative version of such a system, in which the direction is reversed: already extracted and organized pattern sequences give rise to new content, including interpretations, predictions, reproducible states, and new acts of generation. In this sense, a closed system may take the form of a model, an experiment, a technology, a formal law, or even a scientific discipline itself. This content-pattern duality is important because it links the phase of observation and abstraction with the phase of inference and generation. A useful analogy is foreign-language learning: the language itself exists as an open system with its own content, while the learner infers an internal version of that language from extracted patterns and pattern sequences, and then begins to generate new linguistic content from that internalized system. That content is not identical to the original open system, yet it remains understandable to other participants in it and, in the end, naturally becomes part of that open system through the new speaker's individual variant of the same language.

The hypothesis is examined in this paper through two case studies that differ sharply in their observable material but share the same underlying logic. The first is language, represented through Recursive Linguistic Elements as a compositional system of analytical units and higher-order constructions. The second is weather, where observable channels such as temperature, pressure, and wind speed are segmented into time intervals within which these channels can be described by equations that have valid solutions inside the boundaries of each interval.

Taken together, these two cases are used not to claim a complete theory of all complex systems, but to demonstrate that the same methodological logic can operate across domains with very different forms of observable content. The central claim of the paper is that analytical units, abstract patterns, and sequences of patterns may provide a generalizable basis for prediction, interpretation, and discovery in systems whose raw manifestations would otherwise remain too complex to organize directly.

The contribution of this paper is therefore twofold. First, it formulates the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis as a general methodological proposal about how complex systems may be studied through analytical units, abstract patterns, and their sequences. Second, it provides two cross-domain case studies showing how this logic can be implemented in practice in both linguistic and meteorological settings.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. First, the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis and the distinction between open and closed systems are defined in conceptual terms. The two case studies are then presented in turn, followed by a cross-domain discussion of their shared logic, their limits, and their implications for future scientific use of AI in the study of complex systems.
