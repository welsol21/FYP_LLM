In this paper, an open system is understood as a system as it exists in nature, while a closed system is understood as a reconstructed, formalized, or generative version of that system. The distinction between them is therefore not a claim that two different realities exist, but a claim about two different modes in which the same reality may appear in scientific work. In the open mode, the system confronts the observer as external content from which patterns still have to be extracted. In the closed mode, those patterns have already been organized into a form that can produce interpretation, prediction, or new content. Because this organization is always carried out by a particular observer, model, or community of observers, different closed versions of the same open system may coexist. In this sense, the distinction is related to the observer problem known from physics, especially from quantum theory, where the observed system cannot be treated as fully independent of the act of observation: what exists in nature as a field of possible states becomes more definite only through a particular interaction, measurement, or interpretive framework.

From this perspective, open and closed systems should not be understood as mutually isolated domains, but as phases of one cognitive movement. In the open phase, the task is to extract recurring units and regularities from natural content. In the closed phase, the task is to use the resulting pattern organization as a source of inference, reconstruction, controlled generation, prediction, experimental intervention, and, in some cases, discovery of what has not yet been directly observed.

This is precisely the point at which the analogy with Mendeleev becomes methodologically significant. The periodic table was not only a way of arranging known elements, but a closed pattern system from which missing elements could be inferred before they were empirically confirmed. In this sense, the transition from open observation to closed pattern organization is not merely a change in representation; it is the point at which description becomes discovery.

The same logic applies, in a weaker but still meaningful form, to learned or engineered systems. Once recurring patterns have been stabilized into a closed representational form, they can be used not only to reproduce known behaviour, but to generate new admissible states, new interpretations, or new structured hypotheses that remain connected to the original open system from which they were derived.

A useful illustration is foreign-language learning. The language itself exists as an open system with its own content, structures, and constraints. The learner extracts recurring patterns from that open system and organizes them internally into a closed system from which new linguistic content can be generated. That generated content is not identical to the full open system, but insofar as it remains understandable to other speakers and constrained by the same language, it re-enters the open system as part of its ongoing life.

The two case studies developed in this paper can be understood within exactly this dual framework. In language, natural utterances are first encountered as open content and then transformed into Recursive Linguistic Elements and structural pattern sequences. In weather, natural observations such as temperature, pressure, and wind speed are first recorded separately from one another and then transformed into joint equation-based segments and the sequences of those segments, that is, regime sequences. In both cases, the open phase provides the material from which patterns are extracted, while the closed phase provides the organized form from which prediction, interpretation, or discovery becomes possible.
