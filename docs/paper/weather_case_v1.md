The weather case study serves as the first cross-domain test of the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis outside the linguistic domain in which it originally emerged. This matters because weather forecasting differs fundamentally from language in both material and form: instead of symbolic recursive structures, it presents continuous observational signals evolving over time. If the same logic of analytical units, abstract patterns, and pattern sequences remains useful in such a setting, the hypothesis gains stronger methodological support.

In the weather project [REF-WEATHER], the observable content consists of recorded channels such as temperature, pressure, and wind speed. The central methodological move is not to model these channels directly as raw time series, but to analyse each channel separately and segment it into time intervals within which the channel can be described by a local equation. In a second pass, the segmentations obtained from different channels are aligned on a shared temporal axis and refined into joint segments whose boundaries are determined by the point at which at least one of the channel equations ceases to hold. These final segments function as analytical units, from which weather regimes and their sequences can be constructed.

The choice of local equations depends on the physical character of the channel. In the reported experiments, temperature was segmented using six equation families: constant, linear, exponential, harmonic, linear-harmonic, and damped-harmonic. Pressure and wind speed were segmented using three simpler families: constant, linear, and exponential. The purpose of this design was not to reproduce full atmospheric physics, but to capture recurring local forms of behaviour in a compact and interpretable way.

Applied across six years of observations from a single meteorological station, Knock Airport, this two-pass procedure produced a joint corpus of 10,055 segments with a mean duration of 5.3 hours. At this point, the raw stream of hourly measurements had been transformed into a sequence of interpretable analytical units, each representing a period during which all observed channels remained jointly describable within the boundaries of the segment. The forecasting task could therefore be reformulated from predicting raw values directly to predicting the succession of such regimes.

The resulting regime sequence was then used as the input language of the forecasting model. Rather than learning directly from hourly values alone, a transformer was trained to operate over the succession of analytical descriptors associated with the segments and to predict the next regime sequence. In this way, the model learned not only local behaviour types, but also the order in which such types tend to follow one another over time.

The strongest reported result was obtained for pressure forecasting at the 12-hour horizon. On the February 2026 evaluation period, the model achieved a pressure MAE of 0.89 hPa, compared with 1.35 hPa for a naive persistence baseline, corresponding to a 34% improvement. This result is especially important because it shows that, even without explicit atmospheric physics or spatial information, a sequence model operating on analytically segmented regimes can recover predictive structure that is not captured by simple continuation of the last observed state.

This pattern was also visible in temperature and wind speed. For temperature, the ensemble reached MAE values of 1.42 C over hours 1-12, 2.31 C over hours 13-24, and 1.79 C over hours 25-168, with an overall 168-hour MAE of 1.80 C; that is, the forecast differed from observed temperature by about 1.4 C, 2.3 C, 1.8 C, and 1.8 C on average over those intervals. For wind speed, the corresponding MAE values were 2.48 kt, 1.67 kt, and 6.22 kt, with an overall 168-hour MAE of 5.62 kt, meaning that the forecast differed from observed wind speed by about 2.5, 1.7, 6.2, and 5.6 knots on average. For additional evaluation context, these results were also compared with two operational numerical weather prediction baselines: ECMWF IFS 0.25°, the operational forecast system of the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts, and GFS Seamless, the operational forecast product of the U.S. Global Forecast System. This comparison is especially relevant because the present model was developed without the large-scale observational, computational, and operational infrastructure available to the meteorological services behind those systems.

To characterize regime-change timing more directly, a derived turning-point recall was computed on 3-hour smoothed forecast curves using a matching tolerance of plus or minus 6 hours. A change moment was counted as recovered when the forecast contained a turning point within plus or minus 6 hours of the observed turning point.

Although this is not a standard meteorological metric, it provides additional evidence that the segmented regime representation preserves part of the temporal organization of weather evolution. Taken together with the pressure result, this is notable because these results were obtained without the large-scale observational, computational, and operational infrastructure available to the forecasting services used as baselines. The weather case therefore supports the Sequence Pattern Hypothesis beyond the linguistic domain: useful predictive information is retained not only in raw atmospheric measurements, but also in analytically constructed sequences of local regimes.
